Week 5 — Understanding Economic Data Through Statistics and Visualization

Week 5 — Descriptive Statistics and Data Visualization for Economics

Weeks 1–4 focused on:

  • computational setup
  • Python fundamentals
  • programming logic
  • data handling and cleaning

Now students move into:

analytical interpretation

This week introduces:

  • descriptive statistics
  • distributions
  • correlation analysis
  • data visualization
  • economic storytelling with graphs

This is where students begin transforming:

data

into

economic insight

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, students should be able to:

  • generate descriptive statistics
  • interpret averages and variation
  • understand distributions
  • calculate correlations
  • create economic graphs
  • identify patterns and anomalies
  • communicate economic insights visually

2. Why Visualization Matters in Economics

Large datasets are difficult to understand by simply reading tables.

Visualization helps us:

  • detect patterns
  • identify trends
  • communicate evidence
  • compare variables
  • support decisions

Good economists do not merely calculate.

They explain.

Visualization is part of explanation.

Example

Suppose inflation rises from:

  • 4%
  • to 6%
  • to 46%

A table shows numbers.

A graph shows crisis.

That difference matters.

3. Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics summarize the main characteristics of data.

They help answer questions such as:

  • What is the average?
  • How much variation exists?
  • Are values concentrated or spread out?
  • Are there extreme observations?

Common Descriptive Statistics

Statistic Purpose
Mean Average
Median Middle value
Standard deviation Variation
Minimum Lowest value
Maximum Highest value

Generate Descriptive Statistics

data.describe()

Example Interpretation

Statistic Inflation
Mean 12.5
Std 15.2
Max 46.4

Interpretation:

  • Inflation averaged 12.5%
  • Inflation varied substantially
  • Some years experienced very high inflation

4. Measures of Central Tendency

Central tendency describes the “typical” value.

Mean

data["Inflation"].mean()

Median

data["Inflation"].median()

Why Mean and Median Differ

If extreme values exist:

  • the mean may be distorted
  • the median may better represent the “typical” observation

Economic Example

If one household earns extremely high income:

  • average income rises sharply
  • median income changes little

This matters in inequality analysis.

5. Measures of Dispersion

Dispersion measures variability.

Two economies may have the same average inflation but very different stability.

Standard Deviation

data["Inflation"].std()

Interpretation

High standard deviation means:

  • high volatility
  • instability
  • uncertainty

Range

data["Inflation"].max() - data["Inflation"].min()

Economic Significance

Volatility matters for:

  • investment
  • monetary policy
  • household welfare
  • business planning

6. Distributions and Histograms

A distribution shows how values are spread.

Histograms help visualize distributions.

Example Histogram

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

plt.hist(data["Inflation"])

plt.show()

Interpretation Questions

Ask:

  • Is inflation concentrated at low values?
  • Are there extreme observations?
  • Is the distribution symmetric?

Why Distributions Matter

Many statistical methods assume:

  • normality
  • symmetry
  • absence of extreme outliers

Economists must inspect distributions before modeling.

7. Correlation Analysis

Correlation measures how two variables move together.

Example Questions

Do:

  • inflation and exchange rate depreciation move together?
  • unemployment and GDP growth move inversely?

Correlation Coefficient

data["Inflation"].corr(data["Exchange_Rate_Change"])

Correlation Interpretation

Correlation Meaning
+1 Perfect positive relationship
0 No relationship
-1 Perfect negative relationship

Critical Warning

Correlation does NOT imply causation.

This distinction is fundamental in economics.

Example

Ice cream sales and drowning deaths may rise together.

This does NOT mean:

ice cream causes drowning

A third factor:

temperature

drives both.

8. Introduction to Data Visualization

Visualization transforms raw numbers into interpretable patterns.

This week we use:

  • matplotlib
  • Plotly

Import Libraries

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import plotly.express as px

Common Graph Types

Graph Best Use
Line graph Trends over time
Bar chart Comparisons
Scatter plot Relationships
Histogram Distributions

Important Principle

Choose graphs based on:

analytical purpose

not appearance.

9. Line Graphs

Line graphs are ideal for time-series data.

Example

plt.plot(data["Year"], data["Inflation"])

plt.show()

Add Labels

plt.plot(data["Year"], data["Inflation"])

plt.title("Inflation in Sri Lanka")
plt.xlabel("Year")
plt.ylabel("Inflation Rate")

plt.show()

Interpretation

Students should identify:

  • trends
  • spikes
  • turning points
  • crises

10. Bar Charts

Bar charts compare categories.

Example

plt.bar(data["Year"], data["GDP_Growth"])

plt.show()

Economic Uses

Bar charts are useful for:

  • regional comparisons
  • sector comparisons
  • unemployment by gender
  • export composition

Warning

Do not overload bar charts with too many categories.

11. Scatter Plots

Scatter plots help analyze relationships between variables.

Example

plt.scatter(data["Inflation"],
            data["Exchange_Rate_Change"])

plt.show()

Interpretation Questions

Ask:

  • Is there positive relationship?
  • Are there clusters?
  • Are there outliers?

Add Labels

plt.scatter(data["Inflation"],
            data["Exchange_Rate_Change"])

plt.xlabel("Inflation")
plt.ylabel("Exchange Rate Change")

plt.show()

12. Multi-Variable Visualization

Economists often compare several variables simultaneously.

Example: Two-Axis Graph

fig, ax1 = plt.subplots()

ax1.plot(data["Year"], data["Inflation"])

ax2 = ax1.twinx()

ax2.plot(data["Year"], data["Exchange_Rate"])

plt.show()

Interpretation

Students should ask:

  • Do inflation spikes coincide with depreciation?
  • Do variables move together?

Warning

Multi-axis graphs can be misleading.

Use carefully.

13. Interactive Graphs with Plotly

Interactive visualization is increasingly important.

Plotly allows:

  • zooming
  • hovering
  • filtering
  • interaction

Example

fig = px.line(data,
              x="Year",
              y="Inflation",
              title="Inflation Trends")

fig.show()

Why Interactive Graphs Matter

They:

  • improve exploration
  • improve communication
  • improve dashboards
  • support decision-making

14. Telling Economic Stories with Data

Good analysts do not simply create graphs.

They interpret them.

Weak Interpretation

Inflation increased.

Better Interpretation

Inflation remained relatively stable until 2021 but increased sharply in 2022, suggesting severe macroeconomic instability.

Critical Lesson

Graphs without interpretation are incomplete analysis.

21. Common Errors

Problem 1 — Forgetting Labels

Graphs without:

  • titles
  • axis labels
  • units

are poor practice.

Problem 2 — Misinterpreting Correlation

Correlation:

  • indicates association
  • NOT causality

Problem 3 — Overcrowded Graphs

Too many variables create unreadable visualizations.

Simplify.

Problem 4 — Using Wrong Graph Type

Example:

using bar chart for continuous time trends.

Problem 5 — Ignoring Outliers

Extreme observations may:

  • distort averages
  • distort correlations
  • distort conclusions

Always inspect data visually.

22. AI Reflection Question

AI can automatically generate graphs.

However:

  • AI may choose misleading graph types
  • AI may ignore economic context
  • AI may produce visually attractive but analytically weak outputs

Reflection question:

Why is economic interpretation still necessary even when AI tools generate visualizations automatically?
