Week 11 — Forecasting Economic and Business Trends Using Time Series Data

Week 11 — Forecasting and Time Series Analytics for Economics

Weeks 7–10 focused on:

  • regression
  • machine learning
  • classification
  • clustering
  • prediction systems

This week students move into:

forecasting

Forecasting is one of the most important practical applications of:

  • economics
  • statistics
  • machine learning
  • business analytics

Governments, firms, central banks, and international organizations constantly attempt to forecast:

  • inflation
  • GDP growth
  • unemployment
  • exchange rates
  • demand
  • imports and exports
  • financial market movements

This week introduces:

  • time series thinking
  • trend analysis
  • seasonality
  • forecasting models
  • train/test forecasting evaluation
  • forecasting uncertainty
  • forecasting limitations

Critical Conceptual Goal

Students must understand:

Forecasting is not prophecy.

Forecasts are:

  • probabilistic
  • assumption-dependent
  • uncertainty-heavy
  • sensitive to shocks

Good forecasting is about:

disciplined estimation under uncertainty

not:

certainty about the future

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, students should be able to:

  • explain what forecasting is
  • explain why forecasting matters in economics
  • distinguish time series data from cross-sectional data
  • identify trends, seasonality, and cycles
  • visualize time series data
  • calculate moving averages
  • understand train/test splits for forecasting
  • estimate a simple forecasting model
  • evaluate forecast accuracy
  • interpret forecast uncertainty
  • explain why forecasts fail

2. What Is Forecasting?

Forecasting means estimating future values using historical data.

Economists forecast:

  • inflation
  • unemployment
  • GDP growth
  • exchange rates
  • exports
  • imports

Businesses forecast:

  • sales
  • demand
  • inventories
  • customer behavior

Important Lesson

Forecasting is:

  • probabilistic
  • uncertain
  • conditional on assumptions

A forecast may estimate:

Inflation next month is likely between 5% and 7%.

Not:

Inflation will definitely be 6%.

3. Why Forecasting Matters in Economics

Forecasting influences:

  • monetary policy
  • budgeting
  • investment
  • inventory management
  • labor planning
  • international trade decisions

Central Bank Example

Central banks forecast inflation to:

  • decide interest rates
  • assess macroeconomic risks
  • communicate policy expectations

Business Example

A supermarket forecasts demand to:

  • manage inventory
  • reduce shortages
  • reduce waste

Critical Lesson

Bad forecasts can create:

  • policy mistakes
  • financial losses
  • instability
  • overconfidence

4. What Is Time Series Data?

Time series data consists of observations recorded over time.

Examples

Variable Frequency
Monthly inflation Monthly
GDP growth Quarterly
Exchange rate Daily
Sales Weekly

Important Distinction

Cross-Sectional Data

Many observations at one point in time.

Example:

Household survey in 2025

Time Series Data

One variable observed across time.

Example:

Monthly inflation from 2010 to 2025

Example

Year Inflation
2019 4.3
2020 4.6
2021 6.0

Important Lesson

Time creates structure.

Observations are:

  • ordered
  • dependent
  • sequential

This means time series data cannot be treated like ordinary random observations.

5. Trends, Seasonality, and Cycles

Time series often contain:

  • trends
  • seasonality
  • cycles

Trend

A trend is a long-run movement.

Example:

A gradual long-term increase in prices.

Seasonality

Seasonality means regular repeating patterns.

Example:

Tourism peaks during holidays.

Cycles

Cycles are broader fluctuations.

Example:

Business cycles.

Important Lesson

Forecasting models attempt to capture these patterns.

Example Visualization

plt.plot(data["Date"],
         data["Inflation"])

plt.show()

Interpretation Questions

Ask:

  • Is there an upward trend?
  • Are there repeated patterns?
  • Are there structural breaks?
  • Are there crisis periods?

6. Visualizing Time Series

Visualization is critical in forecasting.

Never forecast blindly.

Example

plt.figure(figsize=(10,5))

plt.plot(data["Date"],
         data["Inflation"])

plt.title("Inflation Over Time")
plt.xlabel("Date")
plt.ylabel("Inflation")

plt.show()

Important Lesson

Visualization helps identify:

  • volatility
  • instability
  • outliers
  • crises
  • trend changes

Economic Interpretation

A sudden inflation spike may suggest:

  • exchange rate depreciation
  • supply shocks
  • fiscal pressure
  • monetary instability
  • crisis conditions

The graph is not only technical.

It is economic evidence.

7. Moving Averages

Moving averages smooth fluctuations.

They help reveal underlying trends.

Example

A 3-period moving average:

data["MA_3"] = data["Inflation"].rolling(window=3).mean()

Visualization

plt.plot(data["Inflation"],
         label="Actual")

plt.plot(data["MA_3"],
         label="3-Period Moving Average")

plt.legend()

plt.show()

Important Lesson

Moving averages reduce noise.

But smoothing may hide:

  • shocks
  • turning points
  • crisis signals

Use smoothing carefully.

8. Forecasting Intuition

Forecasting assumes:

past patterns contain useful information about future behavior

This assumption may fail during:

  • crises
  • wars
  • pandemics
  • political instability
  • policy regime changes

Example

Historical inflation patterns may become unreliable during:

severe macroeconomic crises

Critical Lesson

Forecasting models are vulnerable to structural breaks.

A model may perform well in normal periods but fail badly during crises.

9. Train-Test Splits for Time Series

Forecasting evaluation differs from ordinary machine learning.

Important Rule

Future observations must never influence past training.

Correct Logic

Train on earlier periods.

Test on later periods.

Example

Dataset Period
Training 2010–2020
Testing 2021–2022

Example Split

train = data[data["Year"] <= 2020]

test = data[data["Year"] > 2020]

Critical Lesson

Time ordering matters enormously.

Random splitting can create unrealistic forecasting performance.

10. Forecast Evaluation

Forecasts must be evaluated carefully.

Common Metrics

Metric Meaning
MAE Mean Absolute Error
RMSE Root Mean Squared Error
MAPE Percentage forecast error

Import Metric

from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error

Calculate Error

mean_absolute_error(actual, forecast)

Important Lesson

No forecast is perfect.

We evaluate:

  • magnitude of errors
  • stability
  • robustness
  • usefulness

11. Introduction to ARIMA Intuition

ARIMA is one of the most widely used forecasting frameworks.

ARIMA Intuition

ARIMA combines:

  • past values
  • trends
  • previous errors

Important Idea

Future values depend partly on:

previous observations

Example:

If inflation was high recently, future inflation may remain elevated.

Important Warning

Students do NOT need deep mathematical theory this week.

Focus on:

  • intuition
  • workflow
  • interpretation
  • limitations

Import ARIMA

from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA

12. Forecasting with statsmodels

We now estimate a simple forecasting model.

Example

model = ARIMA(train["Inflation"],
              order=(1,1,1))

model_fit = model.fit()

Generate Forecast

forecast = model_fit.forecast(steps=len(test))

Important Lesson

Forecasting models estimate:

expected future paths

not:

certainty

13. Machine Learning Forecasting

Machine learning can also forecast time series.

ML methods may forecast:

  • demand
  • electricity usage
  • inventory needs
  • financial markets
  • sales
  • imports

Important Comparison

Traditional Forecasting ML Forecasting
Strong structure Flexible patterns
More interpretable Often less interpretable
Works with smaller datasets Often needs larger datasets
Theory-guided Performance-guided

Critical Lesson

Complex machine learning forecasting does NOT guarantee better forecasts.

A simple model with good interpretation may be more useful than a complex model that nobody understands.

Interpretation Questions

Ask:

  • Did the forecast capture the trend?
  • Did the forecast miss shocks?
  • Are errors systematic?
  • Is the forecast economically believable?

15. Forecast Uncertainty

All forecasts involve uncertainty.

Important Lesson

Forecasts should often be expressed as:

  • ranges
  • confidence intervals
  • probability distributions

Example

A careful forecast says:

Inflation next quarter is expected between 6% and 8%.

A careless forecast says:

Inflation will be exactly 7%.

Critical Lesson

False certainty is dangerous in forecasting.

Economists should communicate uncertainty clearly.

16. Forecasting Failures and Limitations

Forecasting models frequently fail.

Why?

Because:

  • economies change
  • shocks occur
  • behavior changes
  • crises emerge
  • policy regimes shift

Important Examples

Forecasting failures often occur during:

  • financial crises
  • pandemics
  • political instability
  • wars
  • debt crises

Critical Lesson

Forecasting is useful.

But it is inherently limited.

Good forecasters are humble.

Bad forecasters sound certain.

21. Live Coding Session

Activity Title

Forecasting Inflation Using Time Series Models

Step 1 — Import Libraries

import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error

Step 2 — Load Dataset

data = pd.read_csv("inflation_timeseries.csv")

Step 3 — Inspect Dataset

data.head()
data.info()
data.describe()

Step 4 — Visualize Inflation

plt.figure(figsize=(10,5))

plt.plot(data["Date"],
         data["Inflation"])

plt.title("Inflation Over Time")
plt.xlabel("Date")
plt.ylabel("Inflation")

plt.show()

Step 5 — Create Moving Average

data["MA_3"] = data["Inflation"].rolling(window=3).mean()

Step 6 — Visualize Moving Average

plt.figure(figsize=(10,5))

plt.plot(data["Inflation"],
         label="Actual")

plt.plot(data["MA_3"],
         label="3-Period Moving Average")

plt.legend()

plt.show()

Step 7 — Split Train and Test Sets

train = data.iloc[:-12]

test = data.iloc[-12:]

Step 8 — Estimate ARIMA Model

model = ARIMA(train["Inflation"],
              order=(1,1,1))

model_fit = model.fit()

Step 9 — Generate Forecast

forecast = model_fit.forecast(steps=12)

Step 10 — Evaluate Forecast Error

mean_absolute_error(test["Inflation"],
                    forecast)

Step 11 — Visualize Forecast

plt.figure(figsize=(10,5))

plt.plot(test["Inflation"].values,
         label="Actual")

plt.plot(forecast.values,
         label="Forecast")

plt.legend()

plt.show()

Step 12 — Interpretation Questions

Ask students:

  • Did the forecast capture the inflation trend?
  • Were crisis periods difficult to predict?
  • Why might forecasts fail?
  • What assumptions does forecasting require?
  • Should policymakers rely only on model forecasts?

25. Common Errors

Problem 1 — Random Train-Test Split

Time series must preserve chronological order.

Random splitting creates unrealistic forecasting performance.

Problem 2 — Ignoring Structural Breaks

Forecasts may fail during crises.

A model trained on normal periods may fail during abnormal periods.

Problem 3 — Blind Trust in Forecasts

Forecasts are uncertain estimates.

Do not interpret them as certainty.

Problem 4 — Overcomplicated Models

Complex forecasting models may overfit historical noise.

Problem 5 — Misinterpreting Forecast Accuracy

Low forecast error in stable periods does not guarantee future performance during crises.

26. AI Reflection Question

AI systems can automatically generate forecasts.

However:

  • AI may fail during structural breaks
  • AI may overfit historical patterns
  • AI may ignore political or institutional change
  • AI may produce false confidence

Reflection question:

Why should economists remain cautious even when forecasting models appear highly accurate historically?
